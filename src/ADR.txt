Architecture Design Document.

1. The match() function.

    Why should the match() method be:
    
        Trade match(Order incomingOrder, Side oppositeSide)
    
    and not:
    
        Trade match(Order incomingOrder, PriceLevel restingPL)
    
    → match() needs the best opposite price-level at each iteration/time. So, if a currently executing order 
    fills the head order of a price-level, and it becomes empty, the method will have to get the next valid 
    price-level if it exists. This can be done in two areas:
        1. The while() loop in the submit() function.
        2. Inside the match() function.
    
    Keeping it inside the match() function helps separate the business logic.
    
    Pros and Cons:-

        1. Deriving the PriceLevel inside each seperate function:
            
            Pros: Easy testing
            Cons: Repetitive work in children methods.

        2. Deriving it in submit():
    
            Pros: Reduces resource usage
            Cons: Increases complexity of submit() method, can lead to an erroneous trade if a wrong price-level
            is passed to a concerned method.

    Final decision made to derive it in the submit() function. Main reasons as to why are:

        1. Redundancy: down from three book lookups per iteration to one. That's a real, measurable win,
        not just tidier-looking code.

        2. Optimization: fewer TreeMap traversals per iteration — the same category of cost the spec already
        flagged as "known inefficiency, deferred," except you're addressing it at the design stage instead of
        leaving it as debt.

        3. Separation of business logic: actually improved, not just preserved. Notice that
        crosses(Order, PriceLevel) no longer needs a reference to orderBook at all — it just receives
        data and compares. That makes it more decoupled and more unit-testable than the original delegate,
        which is a stronger version of exactly the principle you invoked to justify the two-layer crosses()
        split in the first place. You've made the delegate purer, not just faster.

    Result:

        Changed methods crosses() and match() to accept a PriceLevel instead of oppositeSide.

2. Who should call removePriceLevel() after a price-level becomes empty? --> submit()

    This was decided to maintain the consistent design decision of seperating responsibilities and the principle
    - each method owning one clear responsibility.

3. Quantity of an Order and in relation - a PriceLeveL:

    IS quantity near Long.MAX_VALUE actually a realistic scenario worth guarding against, or is rejecting
    a single sentinel value (Long.MAX_VALUE exactly) missing the real problem?

    Think about it this way: real exchanges don't solve this by saying "you can't submit exactly Long.MAX_VALUE
    shares." They solve it by having a sane, enforced maximum order size (e.g., no single order can exceed
    some fixed cap, far below any overflow risk) — a business rule, not an overflow workaround.

    the PriceLevel aggregate overflow is practically unreachable for the
    many-small-orders scenario, but that unreachability doesn't come from
    MAX_ORDER_QUANTITY — it comes from ArrayDeque's own limits, independent
    of your cap.

    Fixed quantity to 1 Billion trades per price-level:

    That's a reasonable ballpark, and honestly it's fine to not know this from experience — real exchanges publish these as explicit, documented limits precisely because they're policy decisions, not derived constants. For reference: some real venues do cap single order sizes in the range of millions to tens of millions of shares/contracts for exactly this reason (fat-finger protection, risk control), so 100 million as your estimate of "a very large legitimate order" isn't unreasonable.

    So landing on MAX_ORDER_QUANTITY = 1_000_000_000L (1 billion) gives you roughly 10x headroom above your own estimate of the largest plausible legitimate order — comfortably large enough not to reject anything real, comfortably far from any overflow concern. That's a real justification now, not a placeholder.
